Intraoral scanner

Digital impressions instead of impression trays

The intraoral scanner captures teeth and gums optically and generates a three-dimensional digital model from the data. It replaces the conventional impression with impression material.

Back to overview

How it works

The handheld scanner projects light patterns (structured light) onto the tooth surfaces and captures the reflections with one or more cameras. From a large number of individual frames per second, the software calculates a three-dimensional point cloud in real time and assembles it into a closed surface model. The practitioner follows the progress live on screen and can rescan any missing areas.

The finished model is saved in an open format such as STL or in a manufacturer-specific format. It serves as the basis for computer-aided design (CAD) and manufacturing (CAM) of crowns, bridges, splints or orthodontic appliances – either directly in the practice or in the dental laboratory.

Many scanners offer additional functions such as colour rendering, caries detection using near-infrared light or comparison of scans over time for monitoring. Because no impression material is needed, the procedure is more comfortable for patients, especially those with a strong gag reflex.

Areas of use

  • Digital impressions for crowns, bridges and inlays
  • Planning and progress monitoring in orthodontics
  • Implant prosthetics using scan bodies
  • Fabrication of occlusal splints and temporary restorations
  • Patient education with 3D visualisation

At a glance

Risk class (MDR)
I or IIa depending on design (IIa e.g. with near-infrared caries detection)
Typical settings
Dental practices, orthodontics, implantology, dental laboratories
Space requirement
Mobile cart or laptop version at the treatment chair; no separate room required
Typical service life
around 5–8 years; software updates extend usability

What to look for when procuring

  1. Check whether the scanner exports open file formats (e.g. STL, PLY, OBJ) without additional fees so you can choose freely between laboratories and manufacturing systems.
  2. Clarify the licensing model: some systems charge annual software or cloud fees, others can be used without restriction after purchase – calculate the total cost over several years.
  3. Test the scanner in everyday practice: scanning speed, size and weight of the handpiece and the capture of difficult areas such as deep preparation margins determine acceptance within the team.
  4. Pay attention to hygiene: the scanner tip must be autoclavable or available as a single-use item; calculate the consumable costs.
  5. Plan training for practitioners and assistants and ensure integration with practice software, the laboratory and, where applicable, a milling machine or 3D printer.

Key terms

CAD/CAM
Computer-aided design and manufacturing of dental restorations based on digital data.
STL
Open file format for three-dimensional surface models that can be read by most CAD programs.
Scan body
Screw-on auxiliary component placed on an implant to make its position visible in the scan.
