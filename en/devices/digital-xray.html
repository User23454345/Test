Digital X-ray system

Projection radiography with a digital detector

Digital X-ray systems use X-rays to produce projection images of bones, lungs and other structures; a flat-panel detector delivers the image on screen within seconds.

Back to overview

How it works

An X-ray tube generates radiation by accelerating electrons in a vacuum and letting them strike an anode. The radiation passes through the body and is attenuated to different degrees depending on tissue density: bone absorbs a lot, air in the lungs very little.

Behind the patient, a digital flat-panel detector (DR) measures the remaining intensity; with storage phosphor plates (CR), a cassette is read out afterwards instead. The measurements are converted into a greyscale image that appears on the monitor within seconds and can be digitally post-processed, archived (PACS) and transmitted.

A generator controls tube voltage (kV) and the current-time product (mAs), and thus image quality and dose. Automatic exposure control and collimation limit the dose to what is necessary; an anti-scatter grid improves image contrast but increases the required dose and is therefore only used for thicker body regions. The dose-area product is recorded automatically for documentation.

Areas of use

  • Chest radiographs (lungs, heart)
  • Skeleton and joints (fractures, orthopaedics)
  • Abdominal overview
  • Emergency department and trauma surgery
  • Mobile bedside imaging in intensive care (mobile units)

What to look for when procuring

  1. Choose the detector type: fixed flat-panel detector in table/wall stand or wireless portable detector (Wi-Fi) – the latter is more flexible but vulnerable to drops; clarify insurance or drop protection in the contract.
  2. Radiation protection and licensing: notification or licence from the competent authority under radiation protection law (in Germany: StrlSchG), inspection by an officially appointed expert, structural shielding of the room and staff qualified in radiation protection (appointing a radiation protection officer where required) are prerequisites for operation.
  3. Interfaces: check DICOM (Store, Worklist, MPPS) to PACS/RIS, dose documentation (DICOM RDSR) and connection to the information system.
  4. Quality assurance: plan acceptance and constancy tests (in Germany per DIN 6868); allow for test phantoms and staff time for regular checks.
  5. Check the service contract for tube coverage: the X-ray tube and the detector are the most expensive wear parts; define response times and software updates.

Key terms

Flat-panel detector (DR)
An electronic sensor that converts X-rays directly into a digital image, replacing film and cassettes.
Dose-area product (DAP)
A measure of the radiation delivered to the patient, calculated from dose and irradiated area; it must be documented for every exposure.
Anti-scatter grid
A lead lamella grid in front of the detector that absorbs scattered radiation and thereby improves image contrast.
