Laboratory centrifuge

Separating samples by centrifugal force

A laboratory centrifuge separates blood and other liquids into their components, such as cells and plasma, by rapid rotation. It is an indispensable preparation device for almost every laboratory analysis.

Back to overview

How it works

The sample tubes are placed in a rotor that an electric motor accelerates to several thousand revolutions per minute. The resulting centrifugal force – often many times the force of gravity – pushes heavier components outward or downward. Lighter components remain on top.

In blood, the cells settle at the bottom while clear plasma or serum forms above and is pipetted off for analysis. What matters is not the speed alone but the relative centrifugal force (RCF or g-value), which depends on the speed and the rotor radius.

There are swing-out rotors, in which the tubes swing to a horizontal position during the run, and fixed-angle rotors with permanently inclined positions. Safety features include a lid lock, imbalance detection and, for infectious material, aerosol-tight rotor lids. Refrigerated centrifuges keep the sample at a constant temperature for sensitive analyses.

Areas of use

  • Sample preparation for clinical chemistry and serology
  • Medical practices for obtaining serum and plasma before shipping
  • Blood banks and transfusion medicine
  • Microbiology and research laboratories
  • Urine sediment diagnostics

At a glance

Risk class (MDR/IVDR)
General laboratory equipment; if intended by the manufacturer for in-vitro diagnostics, usually class A under the IVDR
Typical settings
Laboratory, medical practice, blood bank
Space requirement
Benchtop unit; high-performance and refrigerated centrifuges as floor-standing units
Typical service life
Usually 10 years or more with regular maintenance

What to look for when procuring

  1. Choose the rotor and capacity to match your tubes (size, number, samples per run) and the g-value required by your analyses.
  2. For infectious or sensitive material, aerosol-tight buckets or rotor lids and a refrigeration function are advisable.
  3. Look for safety features according to IEC 61010-2-020: lid lock, imbalance cut-off and a stable installation surface with clearance from other equipment.
  4. Consider noise levels and vibration, especially in small practice laboratories, as well as the energy consumption of refrigerated centrifuges.
  5. Plan the regular safety inspections (in Germany: an annual inspection by a competent person in accordance with the DGUV accident-prevention rules), as well as rotor inspections (material fatigue) and documentation of speed verification.

Key terms

Relative centrifugal force (RCF)
Multiple of the acceleration due to gravity (g) acting on the sample; derived from speed and rotor radius and the decisive quantity for separation.
Swing-out rotor
Rotor whose sample buckets swing to a horizontal position during rotation; produces a flat separation layer and is well suited to blood samples.
Fixed-angle rotor
Rotor with permanently inclined tube positions; allows higher speeds and shorter run times.
