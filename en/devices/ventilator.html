Ventilator

Mechanical support or replacement of breathing

A ventilator takes over or supports breathing for patients who cannot breathe sufficiently on their own. It delivers a mixture of air and oxygen into the lungs with controlled pressure and volume.

Back to overview

How it works

The device generates a flow of air and oxygen using valves and either a turbine or a pressurised gas supply. The gas mixture reaches the lungs through a tubing system and an endotracheal tube, a tracheostomy tube or a mask. Exhalation occurs passively through the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall via an expiratory valve.

Sensors continuously measure pressure, flow and volume and regulate ventilation according to the set parameters. Typical settings include respiratory rate, tidal volume, oxygen concentration (FiO2) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). Ventilation modes range from fully controlled ventilation to modes that only assist the patient's spontaneous breathing.

An alarm system monitors limits such as airway pressure, minute volume or disconnection of the tubing system. Humidifiers and filters condition the breathing gas, because dry or cold air can damage the airways. Modern devices display waveforms and measurements on a screen to help assess lung function.

Areas of use

  • Intensive care unit (long-term ventilation)
  • Anaesthesia and recovery room
  • Emergency department and ambulance service (transport ventilation)
  • Home mechanical ventilation
  • Neonatology and paediatrics

What to look for when procuring

  1. Clarify the area of use: intensive care, transport and home ventilation place different demands on ventilation modes, battery life and weight.
  2. Check the gas supply: turbine-driven devices only need oxygen, while pneumatically driven devices also require medical compressed air; the wall outlets must be compatible.
  3. Consider the consumables (tubing systems, filters, humidifier chambers) and whether single-use or reprocessable systems are more economical.
  4. Pay attention to interfaces with the patient data management system (PDMS) and central alarm forwarding.
  5. Plan for user training under the German Medical Devices Operator Ordinance (MPBetreibV), periodic safety inspections (STK) and a service contract with guaranteed response times; loan devices for downtime are advisable.

Key terms

PEEP
Positive end-expiratory pressure: a residual pressure at the end of exhalation that keeps the alveoli open.
Tidal volume
The volume of gas delivered to the lungs with each breath.
FiO2
Fraction of oxygen in the inhaled gas mixture (room air: 21 %).
