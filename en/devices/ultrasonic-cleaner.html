Ultrasonic Cleaner

Pre-cleaning by cavitation in a liquid bath

Ultrasonic cleaners use high-frequency sound waves in a cleaning bath to loosen even stubborn soil from joints, grooves and cavities of instruments and complement automated reprocessing.

Back to overview

How it works

Transducers on the bottom or walls of the stainless-steel tank convert electrical energy into sound waves, usually at frequencies of 35 to 45 kilohertz. These waves generate tiny vacuum bubbles in the cleaning liquid that implode within fractions of a second. This process is called cavitation.

The implosions act like millions of tiny brushes and loosen blood, tissue residues and cement deposits even in places that cannot be reached mechanically, such as joints, serrations and lumens. A detergent in the bath matched to the instrument material supports the process chemically, and heating to about 40 °C improves the effect without fixing proteins.

The instruments lie open in a mesh basket that keeps them off the tank bottom and are rinsed thoroughly after a few minutes. The ultrasonic bath is a pre-cleaning step: the subsequent disinfection and sterilization take place in the washer-disinfector and the sterilizer. The liquid must be degassed after each fresh fill, and the bath must be changed regularly – usually every working day; effectiveness can be checked with foil or indicator tests.

Areas of use

  • Pre-cleaning of surgical instruments with joints and fine structures
  • Dental practices: burs, milling tools, root canal instruments
  • Ophthalmic and ENT surgery with microsurgical instruments
  • Laboratories and workshops for prosthetics and dental technology

At a glance

Risk class (MDR)
usually I (cleaning device, MDR Rule 1); IIa if the manufacturer claims a disinfecting effect (Rule 16)
Typical settings
Dirty side of the reprocessing room in practices and CSSD
Space requirement
Benchtop unit; tank volume usually 3 to 30 litres, footprint from approx. 30 × 30 cm
Typical service life
approx. 8 to 10 years

What to look for when procuring

  1. Match tank volume and basket dimensions to the longest instruments and to the batch volume; instruments must be fully submerged without touching each other or overloading the basket.
  2. Check the features: an adjustable timer, heating with a temperature limit, a degassing function, a lid to contain aerosols and a drain tap make operation easier and protect staff.
  3. Choose suitable cleaning chemicals: use only detergents approved for ultrasonic use and for the instrument materials (stainless steel, aluminium, plastics) and document the dosing and the in-use time of the bath.
  4. Integrate the ultrasonic step into the validated reprocessing chain: follow the instrument manufacturers' instructions, draw up a work instruction and verify effectiveness regularly with test pieces.
  5. Expect low purchase costs, but budget for wear parts such as the tank, transducers and lid as well as the ongoing need for detergent and demineralized water; consider noise protection in continuous operation.

Key terms

Cavitation
Formation and implosion of microscopic vapour bubbles in a liquid caused by ultrasound; the energy released loosens soil from surfaces.
Degassing
Short idle run of the bath after filling so that dissolved air escapes; dissolved gases dampen cavitation and reduce cleaning performance.
Transducer
Piezoelectric component that converts the alternating voltage of the generator into mechanical vibrations of the tank.
