Magnetic resonance imaging scanner (MRI)

Cross-sectional imaging with a magnetic field and radio waves

The MRI scanner images soft tissue, brain, joints and organs without X-rays – using a strong magnetic field, radio waves and excellent soft-tissue contrast.

Back to overview

How it works

A superconducting magnet generates a very strong, constant magnetic field (clinically mostly 1.5 or 3 tesla). The hydrogen nuclei (protons) in the body align within it. Short radiofrequency pulses tip the protons out of alignment; as they relax back they emit a weak signal that receiver coils pick up.

Gradient coils slightly vary the magnetic field in space so that each signal can be assigned to a location – this causes the typical loud knocking noises. Depending on the tissue, the signal decays at different rates (T1 and T2 relaxation); this yields sequences with different contrast that complement each other.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents enhance vessels and inflammatory or tumorous processes. Special sequences enable diffusion imaging (e.g. early signs of stroke), angiography without contrast and functional imaging. The magnet is permanently active – even when the system is switched off – so strict access rules are essential.

Areas of use

  • Neurology (brain, spinal cord)
  • Musculoskeletal diagnostics (joints, intervertebral discs)
  • Oncology (prostate, breast, liver)
  • Cardiac MRI and MR angiography
  • Abdomen and pelvis

What to look for when procuring

  1. Choose field strength and bore according to the examination spectrum: 1.5 T covers routine work, 3 T provides more signal for neuro and prostate imaging; a wide bore (70 cm) improves comfort for obese and claustrophobic patients.
  2. Structural requirements are considerable: RF shielding (Faraday cage), magnetic shielding, floor loads of several tonnes, a quench pipe to the outside and a transport opening for the magnet – involve specialist planners early.
  3. Running costs: compare helium refilling or low-helium systems, power consumption, cooling and contrast medium; check that the full-service contract covers the cold head and the coils.
  4. Assemble a coil package to match the examination spectrum (head, spine, joints, body, breast) – coils are expensive and largely determine image quality.
  5. Safety and operator duties: plan an MR safety concept with zones, staff training, screening of implants/devices for MR safety (MR safe or MR conditional) and MR-safe accessories (anaesthesia, monitoring, infusion pumps). In Germany, MRI scanners fall under Annex 1 of the MPBetreibV: training by the manufacturer or an authorised person, a medical device logbook and regular safety inspections are mandatory.

Key terms

Tesla (T)
Unit of magnetic flux density. Clinical MRI systems typically operate at 1.5 or 3 T – tens of thousands of times stronger than the Earth's magnetic field.
Sequence (T1/T2)
A defined series of radiofrequency and gradient pulses. T1-weighted images show anatomy well, T2-weighted images highlight fluid and inflammation.
Quench
The sudden loss of superconductivity, in which the liquid helium evaporates abruptly; the gas must be vented outside through a quench pipe.
